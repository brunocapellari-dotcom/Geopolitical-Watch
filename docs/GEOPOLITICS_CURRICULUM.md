# Geopolitical Watch — Deep Geopolitics Curriculum

This curriculum is the permanent knowledge-development program for the Geopolitical Watch analytical layer. It is not a substitute for source verification. Knowledge produced here is **analytical context**, never factual evidence that an event occurred.

## Learning objective

Build a multidisciplinary analytical capability comparable to advanced graduate/postgraduate work in international relations, strategic studies, political geography, international political economy, security studies, military history and international law, while remaining operationally useful for real-time conflict monitoring.

## Methodological rule

Every material geopolitical conclusion should be examinable through at least three lenses where relevant:

1. material/power explanation;
2. institutional/economic explanation;
3. ideational/domestic-political explanation.

The system must record disagreement between theories rather than silently collapsing them into one narrative.

## Program 1 — Foundations of international relations

- Sovereignty, statehood, anarchy and international order.
- Power: material, economic, military, technological, institutional and soft power.
- National interest and grand strategy.
- Polarity, balancing, bandwagoning and alliance formation.
- Security dilemma, offense-defense balance and misperception.
- Deterrence, compellence, coercive diplomacy and bargaining.
- Cooperation, international institutions and regimes.
- Identity, norms, legitimacy and strategic culture.
- International society and great-power management.
- Critical geopolitics and the politics of representation.

Canonical lenses: classical realism, structural realism, liberal institutionalism, constructivism, English School, critical approaches and geoeconomics.

## Program 2 — Classical and contemporary geopolitics

- Sea power and maritime access.
- Land power, interior lines and strategic depth.
- Heartland, Rimland and their historical limits.
- Chokepoints, straits, canals and maritime corridors.
- Buffers, spheres of influence and contested borderlands.
- Geography of energy, minerals, food and water.
- Infrastructure as geopolitical power: ports, pipelines, railways, grids, subsea cables, satellites and data centers.
- Arctic access and climate-driven route change.
- Space and cyber as strategic domains.

Historical theorists are studied as intellectual tools, not deterministic laws of geography.

## Program 3 — War, peace and strategic studies

- Causes of war and bargaining failures.
- Private information and incentives to misrepresent.
- Commitment problems and preventive-war logic.
- Indivisibility and symbolic stakes.
- Escalation ladders and inadvertent escalation.
- Crisis bargaining and signaling.
- Conventional deterrence.
- Nuclear deterrence, second strike and strategic stability.
- Arms races, force posture and mobilization.
- Military doctrine, readiness and force employment.
- Limited war, attrition, maneuver and exhaustion.
- Air, land, maritime, missile and drone campaigns at an analytical level.
- Insurgency, counterinsurgency and civil war.
- Proxy warfare and external sponsorship.
- Peace agreements, ceasefires and conflict recurrence.

The system must distinguish military capability indicators from actual intent.

## Program 4 — Political economy and geoeconomics

- Trade dependence and asymmetric interdependence.
- Sanctions, embargoes, export controls and asset freezes.
- Financial networks, payment systems and reserve currencies.
- Technology controls and strategic industries.
- Friend-shoring, de-risking and decoupling.
- Foreign direct investment and geopolitical blocs.
- Industrial policy and defense-industrial capacity.
- Energy security, OPEC+, LNG and refining.
- Food security, grains and fertilizers.
- Critical minerals and processing concentration.
- Shipping, insurance, freight and rerouting.
- Economic coercion, evasion and substitution.

Economic statecraft must be analyzed through intended mechanism, actual implementation, evasion paths, second-order effects and distributional costs.

## Program 5 — International law and institutions

- UN Charter and prohibition on the use of force.
- Self-defense and collective self-defense.
- UN Security Council and collective security.
- Sanctions regimes.
- Treaty interpretation and legal claims.
- International humanitarian law and conflict classification.
- Distinction, proportionality and precautions.
- Protected persons and objects.
- Occupation law at a high-level analytical overview.
- International criminal accountability as a separate legal layer.

Legal claims are not to be inferred from political preference. The system must distinguish: legal rule, asserted legal basis, observed facts and unresolved legal dispute.

## Program 6 — Domestic politics and foreign policy

- Regime type and foreign-policy constraints.
- Elections, coalitions and public opinion.
- Bureaucratic politics and civil-military relations.
- Regime security and elite survival.
- Nationalism, religion and identity.
- Economic interests and lobbying.
- Leadership beliefs and cognitive bias.
- Coup risk, state fragility and institutional capacity.

Domestic explanations should not be used as catch-all explanations without evidence.

## Program 7 — Information, intelligence and uncertainty

- Source reliability versus claim confidence.
- Confirmation bias and motivated reasoning.
- Deception, denial, propaganda and strategic narratives.
- Information operations and disinformation.
- OSINT limitations.
- Missing data and survivorship bias.
- Base rates and historical analogy.
- Competing hypotheses.
- Red-team analysis.
- Forecast calibration and Brier scores.
- Bayesian updating as a disciplined reasoning framework, not a false-precision generator.

## Program 8 — Regional systems

### Europe and Eurasia
NATO-Russia security interaction, Black Sea, Eastern Europe, Caucasus, sanctions, energy routes and post-Soviet state formation.

### Middle East
Regional deterrence, Iran-Arab competition, Israel-Palestine, proxy networks, Gulf security, Hormuz, Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb and energy markets.

### Indo-Pacific
US-China competition, Taiwan Strait, South China Sea, Korean Peninsula, Japan, India, ASEAN, Malacca and technology supply chains.

### Africa
Sahel, Horn of Africa, Red Sea, Great Lakes, state capacity, insurgencies, external security partnerships, minerals and ports.

### Latin America
Regional institutions, resource geopolitics, organized political violence, border disputes, extra-regional powers and commodity exposure.

### Arctic
Climate change, new routes, bases, resources and military access.

## Program 9 — Historical case-study laboratory

At minimum the system maintains structured comparative studies of:

- Peloponnesian War;
- First World War;
- Second World War;
- early Cold War and Berlin crises;
- Korean War;
- Suez Crisis;
- Cuban Missile Crisis;
- 1973 oil shock;
- Soviet-Afghan War;
- Iran-Iraq War;
- Gulf War 1990-91;
- Yugoslav wars;
- Afghanistan after 2001;
- Iraq after 2003;
- Georgia 2008;
- Crimea/Donbas 2014;
- Syrian civil war;
- Red Sea shipping disruptions from 2023 onward.

Every analogy must state both similarities and analogy limits.

## Program 10 — Quantitative geopolitical indicators

- Military expenditure and burden.
- Arms transfers.
- Force structure and readiness proxies.
- Conflict event frequency and lethality.
- Governance and state capacity.
- Trade concentration.
- FDI concentration.
- Energy dependency.
- Shipping connectivity and chokepoint exposure.
- Sanctions coverage.
- Exchange-rate and sovereign-risk stress.
- Refugee/displacement flows.
- Food and fertilizer dependence.

Indicators never speak for themselves; definitions, revisions, uncertainty and source methodology must accompany them.

## Program 11 — Analytical products

The knowledge layer should support:

- country strategic profiles;
- bilateral rivalry profiles;
- alliance/network profiles;
- strategic infrastructure profiles;
- conflict episode context notes;
- escalation assessments;
- scenario trees;
- sanctions/geoeconomic impact notes;
- historical analogies with limitations;
- early-warning indicators;
- downstream consumer feeds.

## Program 12 — Continuous learning loop

1. New episode enters evidence layer.
2. Entity and geography resolution identifies relevant knowledge nodes.
3. Knowledge engine retrieves concepts, regional themes and comparable cases.
4. Analytical context is attached with `context_not_evidence=true`.
5. Analyst-review queue captures uncertain or novel patterns.
6. Resolved review decisions create candidate knowledge updates.
7. Candidate update requires source citation and contradiction check.
8. Knowledge version increments only after tests and review.
9. Historical outcomes are later compared with prior analytical expectations.
10. Forecast and alert calibration metrics update future weighting.

## Canonical knowledge-source hierarchy

1. Primary legal texts and official government/intergovernmental documents.
2. Transparent official/research datasets with methodology.
3. Peer-reviewed or academically established literature.
4. Specialist research institutes with disclosed methods.
5. High-quality journalism for current facts and elite statements.
6. Social media only as a signal requiring corroboration.

Books and copyrighted research are stored only as bibliographic references, structured notes and original summaries; full copyrighted text is not copied into the repository.

## Promotion standard

A topic is considered mature only when the system has:

- definitions and competing theoretical explanations;
- canonical sources;
- historical cases;
- measurable indicators where possible;
- known failure modes;
- explicit uncertainty;
- regional applications;
- testable analytical questions;
- documented links to episode analysis;
- revision history.
